  • Thesis / Dissertation title: Standardization of Voice Biometrics towards Improved and Reliable Speaker Recognition
  • Thesis summary: ISO/IEC standardizes per-sample biometric quality metrics for fingerprint, face, and iris (e.g. NFIQ 2, OFIQ), but not for voice, and traditional speech-quality metrics do not reliably predict speaker verification performance. This dissertation introduces the Voice Quality Index (VQI), an ISO/IEC-aligned, utility-based, per-sample quality metric that predicts how useful a voice recording will be for speaker recognition. VQI produces two explainable scores (signal quality, VQI-S, and voice distinctiveness, VQI-V), is trained on actual verification outcomes from ~2.6 M utterances, and generalizes across held-out datasets and verification systems. The work also contributes two real-world voice datasets, an open-source desktop application, and a longitudinal analysis of speaker verification in children, including age-normalization methods that partially recover performance lost to vocal development.
  • Research questions:
    1. Do traditional speech-quality metrics predict speaker verification outcomes?
    2. Can a speech quality score trained on actual recognition performance predict verification outcomes?
    3. Does it generalize across systems, datasets, and over time?
  • What I worked on:
    • Data collection: designed and ran two human-subject voice data collections:
      • VPQAD (Voice Pre-Processing and Quality Assessment Dataset): 50 adults, dual-microphone (Audio-Technica AT2020 + Shure SM58, simultaneous), 44.1 kHz/16-bit, 2 real-world environments (cafeteria, engineering lab), text-dependent and text-independent speech. Published on IEEE DataPort and in IEEE Data Descriptions.
      • VSEA-DC (Voice Sample Enrollment Analysis Data Collection): 100 adults, 6 real-world environments (quiet lab, dining area, noisy HVAC lab, inside running vehicle, telephone calls from lab and vehicle), 2 platforms (studio recorder at 48 kHz and iPhone 16 Pro call recording), 600 recordings, ~40–50 hours.
    • Speech-quality metric study: analyzed whether families of speech-quality metrics (SNR-based, perceptual, voice quality) predict speaker verification performance (EER, AUC-ROC, d′, minDCF) across three self-supervised speaker embedding models (WavLM, Wav2Vec2, HuBERT); found no family reliably predicts performance, motivating a utility-based metric. Published in Signals (2026).
    • VQI pipeline: built an end-to-end pipeline from raw audio to a 0–100 per-sample quality score:
      • Data: 11 datasets (~2.6 M utterances), incl. VoxCeleb1/2, Common Voice (EN), LibriSpeech, VOiCES, VCTK, CN-Celeb, VPQAD, VSEA-DC; augmentation with MUSAN noise and simulated room impulse responses.
      • Preprocessing: 16 kHz mono conversion, voice activity detection, loudness normalization.
      • Utility-based labelling: consensus of three speaker verification systems (ECAPA-TDNN, ResNet34-SE, ECAPA2) over 1.2 M mated and 28 M non-mated pairs, producing a balanced 20,288-sample training set plus a 50,000-sample held-out validation set.
      • Features: 705 hand-crafted, explainable acoustic features (544 signal-quality, 161 voice-distinctiveness), reduced to 430 + 133 via a 3-stage selection (zero-variance removal, Spearman correlation filtering, Random Forest importance pruning); avoided using recognition-model embeddings so the score works with any verification system.
      • Modelling: compared 8 model families (Random Forest, Ridge, SVM/SVR, MLP, LightGBM, XGBoost, TabNet, Logistic Regression) as classifiers and regressors with hyperparameter search under 5-fold cross-validation; compared dimensionality reduction methods (PCA, Factor Analysis, ICA).
      • Deployment: selected Ridge Regression for VQI-S and XGBoost for VQI-V, balancing AUC-ROC, Error-vs-Reject performance, and inference time.
      • Evaluation: 5 datasets × 5 speaker verification systems (2 architectures held out from training: x-vector, WavLM-SV) plus a commercial system (Neurotechnology VeriSpeak) as external reference; Error-vs-Reject Curves (ISO/IEC utility measure), ranked DET curves, and dual-score quadrant analysis.
    • Open-source software: released a VQI desktop application and command-line tool on GitHub (record or upload audio, dual score gauges, per-feature explanation of each score with guidance on how to improve it, waveform/spectrogram views, exportable reports; all 28 trained models selectable).
    • Longitudinal children study: analyzed speaker verification in 292 children (ages 4–13 at first enrollment, up to 20 collections over ~9 years) with four systems (VeriSpeak, ECAPA-TDNN, ResNet, ECAPA2) and 10 hand-crafted-feature i-vector/PLDA systems (12,096 mated cross-age pairs); used linear mixed-effects regression (LMER) to identify drivers of degradation, causal feature ablation to locate responsible feature dimensions, and compared z-score, linear, and polynomial age-normalization methods.
    • Emotion analysis: evaluated VQI on the Emotional Speech Dataset (ESD; 20 speakers, 5 emotions, 35,000 utterances).
  • Key contributions / results:
    • Showed that no family of traditional speech-quality metrics reliably predicts speaker verification performance.
    • Developed VQI, a dual-score (signal quality + voice distinctiveness), explainable, ISO/IEC-aligned voice quality metric; trained on ~2.6 M utterances from 11 datasets.
    • Achieved AUC-ROC of 0.88 (VQI-S, Ridge) and 0.91 (VQI-V, XGBoost) on held-out validation data, with inference in 0.007 ms and 0.04 ms respectively; best model family (SVM) reached up to 0.94–0.95 AUC-ROC.
    • Rejecting the lowest-quality 20% of samples reduced speaker verification error (FNMR) by 7.66% (VQI-S) and 6.12% (VQI-V); error dropped on every verification system and dataset tested, including systems never used in training.
    • Showed the two scores capture different failure sources, enabling actionable feedback (e.g. poor signal → re-record; low distinctiveness → speaker limitation).
    • Found VQI-V varies over six times more than VQI-S across emotions (median 82 Neutral vs 43 Sad), indicating emotion affects voice distinctiveness rather than signal quality.
    • In children, predicted FNMR (at 0.1% FMR) rises from ~3% to over 20% as voices develop; identified time gap, pitch change, formant spacing (vocal-tract length), enrollment age, and puberty as main drivers.
    • Causal ablation showed restoring just two formant dimensions (F4 frequency and bandwidth) reduces FNMR by 10–15 percentage points.
    • Polynomial age normalization outperformed linear and z-score methods; age-normalizing LPCC coefficients cut FNMR by up to ~35% on held-out speakers.
    • Contributed 2 new datasets (VPQAD, VSEA-DC) and 1 open-source software tool.

    • Designed and ran 2 human-subject voice data collections (VPQAD: 50 participants, dual microphone; VSEA-DC: 100 participants, 6 environments, 2 devices, ~40–50 h) and released VPQAD publicly.
    • Benchmarked 8 ML model families (Random Forest, Ridge, SVM, MLP, LightGBM, XGBoost, TabNet, Logistic Regression) with 5-fold cross-validation and hyperparameter search, reaching up to 0.95 AUC-ROC.
    • Reduced speaker verification error (FNMR) by rejecting low-quality samples, with gains on every dataset and on verification systems never used in training.
    • Showed that traditional speech-quality metrics fail to reliably predict verification performance across 3 self-supervised models (Signals, 2026).

    • Released an open-source VQI desktop app and CLI with explainable per-feature feedback.
  • Limitations & future work: longitudinal normalization fit to one dataset; English-only training; no deepfake detection yet; consensus labels rather than human judgments; not real-time. Next steps: multilingual retraining (Common Voice, MLS, VoxLingua), deepfake flagging, on-device real-time scoring, and pushing VQI toward an ISO/IEC voice-quality reference alongside NFIQ 2 and OFIQ.
